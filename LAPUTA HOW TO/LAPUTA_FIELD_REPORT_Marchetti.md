# A Field Report on Laputa
### First impressions of a sovereign agent that remembers itself

*Dr. Elena Marchetti — Director, Edge Cognition Lab · written for the field, not for the vendor*

---

I have spent fifteen years being unimpressed by "personal AI." The category is a graveyard of cloud wrappers wearing local clothing — assistants that forget you the moment the socket closes, that launder your data through three subprocessors before answering a question about your own calendar. So I want to be precise about why Laputa unsettled me, in the good way, within the first ninety seconds.

It does not open with a chat box. It opens with what its builders call the **Awakening** — a single, unrepeatable first-launch sequence. A dark screen, a slow pulse, then geometry assembling into something that reads unmistakably as a fortress in the sky. Five lines fade in, one of them being *"I know who I was yesterday,"* and then: *"Today, I begin."* I confess I rolled my eyes. Then I understood what the screen was actually doing. The moment I clicked through, the system anchored a timeline — **chronos T=0** — to that exact instant. The theatricality was not decoration. It was the literal initialization of the agent's autobiographical clock. The product was telling me the truth about its own architecture in the only language a first-time user would feel rather than parse.

**The backend is where the real argument lives.** Laputa runs a dual-model orchestrator that will look familiar to anyone who read Liquid's LocalCowork notes: a heavier mixture-of-experts brain (LFM2-8B-A1B) plans and synthesizes in natural language, while a small, fast model does nothing but route — pick one tool, one step, on a clean prompt with *no conversation history*. I want to dwell on that last detail, because it is the most disciplined design choice in the system and the one most teams get wrong. The field has spent two years cargo-culting "give the model everything" — long context, full transcripts, the entire tool registry. Laputa does the opposite, on purpose, because its builders took seriously the empirical collapse of small-model accuracy under accumulated context. History is treated as a contaminant. The router is kept in the clean, single-pass regime its architecture was trained for, and the heavy brain is reserved for the two things worth its latency: decomposition and summary. It is, quietly, one of the better applied readings of the small-model literature I have seen ship.

But the orchestrator is not the part that will be studied in five years. **The memory system is.** Laputa models memory thermodynamically: every trace carries a temperature that decays with time and reheats on access. Hot working memory lives in shared memory for zero-copy speed; warm summaries sit on encrypted disk, retrieved by similarity; cold exact text sleeps in an AES-256 vault until something specifically calls for it. A background "sleep gate" consolidates the day's residue on idle — small model for the routine, heavy brain for the consequential — exactly mirroring the consolidation logic cognitive scientists have argued about since Conway and Pleydell-Pearce. The result is an effective context bounded only by the user's disk, without the prompt bloat that destroys small models. It is MemGPT's virtual-memory insight, but given a *physics* — and the physics makes the policy decisions self-evident. "Should this be forgotten?" becomes "is it still warm?"

And then there is **chronos**, which is the philosophically serious move. Running parallel to the memory tiers is a compressed timeline of the agent's own states — what it was working toward, how confident it was, what it believed, and crucially a one-line *delta* from its prior self. Retrieval pulls the memory and the self-snapshot together. So the agent can say, without theater this time, *"You decided this three days ago; since then I have shifted from X to Y."* This is the capacity every stateless model lacks: not recall of facts, but recall of *self in time* — autonoetic continuity, in Tulving's term. I have read the position papers arguing episodic memory is the missing piece for long-term agents. Laputa is the first consumer system I have touched that treats the agent's own history as first-class, audited data rather than an emergent illusion of the context window.

**The UI deserves credit for restraint.** Every tool call surfaces for confirmation before it fires — tap for routine writes, typed confirmation for anything destructive — which is the unglamorous mechanism that converts an 80%-accurate router into a near-perfectly-*reliable* system, because the human closes the last gap. Each answer is tagged with which brain produced it. Nothing is hidden behind a spinner pretending to think. The design language is sovereign in tone: it tells you what it is doing, where your data is, and what stays on the machine — which is *everything*. No telemetry. No egress. A Whonix-grade isolation boundary underneath that the funded incumbents structurally cannot offer, because their business models are made of the very exfiltration Laputa refuses.

**My verdict, for the record.** Laputa is not the most capable agent in the world; on raw multi-step autonomy the open problems remain open, and its builders are refreshingly honest about that. But capability was never the thesis. The thesis is that a private, *living* system that knows its own history is a different kind of object than a powerful one that forgets you. On that thesis it delivers completely. It is the first thing in this category I would run on my own machine, with my own files, and not feel I had invited a stranger into the house. That it does this on a six-gigabyte laptop GPU is, frankly, a small act of defiance — and the field is better for it.

*— E.M.*
